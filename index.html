    🧮 How the checker works

    The overall light is the worst of four separate checks, so one serious risk is never hidden by good numbers elsewhere.

    • Pavement: in direct sun, asphalt can reach about 125°F when the air is 77°F and about 143°F at 87°F (figures published by the Vets Now emergency veterinary service). The checker draws a straight line through those two points to estimate the pavement in full sun and uses half of that extra heat for partly sunny skies. Skin can be damaged in about a minute at 125°F, so 125°F and above is red and 115°F to 124°F is yellow. It is an estimate: dark asphalt, time of day and surface type all change the real number.
    • Heat: when you add humidity, the checker uses the National Weather Service heat index formula (the Rothfusz regression with its official adjustments). The NWS calls 80°F to 90°F Caution, 90°F to 103°F Extreme Caution and 103°F and above Danger. Dogs cool themselves less well than people, so 80°F and above is yellow, 90°F and above is red for dogs with a risk factor (flat face, senior or puppy, overweight, heart or breathing condition, thick coat) and 103°F and above is red for every dog. Flat-faced dogs get a yellow from 75°F.
    • Cold: the checker uses the National Weather Service wind chill (defined at 50°F or colder with wind above 3 mph). Veterinary guidance says some dogs feel the cold below 45°F, small, thin-coated, very young, old or unwell dogs are at risk below 32°F, and any dog can get hypothermia or frostbite below 20°F. Following the Tufts Animal Care and Condition scale notes, a thick or double coat relaxes the result by one level, and rain, sleet or snow makes it one level stricter.
    • Air quality: the EPA air quality index categories: 0 to 50 Good, 51 to 100 Moderate, 101 to 150 Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups, 151 and above Unhealthy or worse. Senior dogs, puppies, flat-faced dogs and dogs with heart or breathing conditions are treated as sensitive.

    Sources: National Weather Service heat index equation and wind chill formula; Vets Now, hot pavements; PetMD cold-weather guidance; Tufts Animal Care and Condition scales; EPA AirNow AQI basics. Checked September 30, 2026.

    ❓ Frequently asked questions

    How hot is too hot to walk a dog?

    It depends on heat, humidity and your dog. Take extra care once the heat index reaches 80°F, and skip strenuous walks at 90°F and above for flat-faced, senior, overweight or thick-coated dogs. In direct sun the pavement can be far hotter than the air: about 125°F when the air is 77°F.

    How can I tell if the pavement is too hot?

    Use the 7-second test: press the back of your hand on the pavement for 7 seconds. If it is too hot for you to hold, it is too hot for paw pads. Walk on grass or in shade, or go early in the morning or late in the evening.

    How cold is too cold for a walk?

    Below 45°F some dogs start to feel the cold. Below 32°F, small, thin-coated, very young, old or unwell dogs are at risk, and below 20°F any dog can develop hypothermia or frostbite with long exposure. Wind makes it feel colder, so the checker uses the wind chill.

    Where does the weather come from?

    You can type it in, or press Use my location to fill it from the National Weather Service hourly forecast, which covers US locations only. Your location is sent to the National Weather Service only when you press the button.

    Is this veterinary advice?

    No. It is general guidance. Heavy panting, drooling, stumbling, vomiting or collapse can be signs of heatstroke, and shivering, lifting paws or slowing down can mean it is too cold. Call your veterinarian or an emergency clinic right away if you are worried.

    ⚠️ Please note

    This checker is general guidance, not veterinary advice. Every dog is different, so watch yours closely. If you see heavy panting, drooling, stumbling, vomiting or collapse, move your dog to shade, offer water and call a veterinarian or emergency clinic right away.
